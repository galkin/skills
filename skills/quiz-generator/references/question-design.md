# Question design

The craft every item and every set is held to. Run each candidate through **Item gates**, then the whole set through **Set review**.

## One decision per question

Each question assesses one **decision**: the reader weighs a constraint and picks a course of action, a diagnosis, or a distinction that matters in production. A synthesis question may draw on several lessons, still in service of one decision. Prefer application, diagnosis and evaluation; use recall only where the objective in scope asks for it, and never to raise the count — acronym expansions and product trivia are not decisions.

Before writing an item, pin down its design check, and keep it in your working notes:

- the decision it assesses and the lesson (or objective) that teaches it;
- the **deciding constraint** — the fact in the prompt that makes one answer best;
- the best answer, and for each distractor the mistaken reasoning that leads to it.

Write new questions. Use ecosystem-native product terms exactly as the vendor does; originality is in the scenario, not in renaming things. Questions recalled from real exams or dumps are out.

## The prompt

- A short scenario carrying the requirements and constraints that decide the answer, and nothing that does not.
- A criterion for any "best", "most appropriate" or "first": preserve a trust boundary, meet a stated latency budget, satisfy the constraints with the least operational effort. The reader never has to guess an unstated preference for a product or pattern.
- Plain English for an international technical audience. Difficulty comes from reasoning; obscure terms and missing facts are not difficulty.
- A simple conceptual distinction is asked plainly, without a fictional company around it.
- Positive stems. When a negative stem is unavoidable, the negation is **bold**.

## The choices

- Exactly one correct choice.
- **Four by default.** Three when a fourth would be filler, two only for a genuine binary distinction, five only when every extra alternative diagnoses a different mistake.
- Each distractor is a **recognisable mistake**: a sound approach in the wrong context, a missing boundary, a solution to the neighbouring problem, confused responsibilities, an incomplete fix.
- **Parallel** in grammar, abstraction, specificity and length, so that no choice is the **odd one out** — the only one that opens with a different word, is built differently, carries a justification (`since …`, `so that …`), or runs clearly longer or shorter than the rest. Read the choices with the prompt covered: an odd one out that is correct gives the answer away, and one that is a distractor is eliminated without thought. Rewrite until none stands apart.
- Choices are mutually exclusive and distinct in meaning; "all/none of the above" and absolutes used as elimination cues do not appear.
- Try to defend every distractor under the scenario as written. If a prepared reader could defend one as equally good, repair the constraint or drop the item.

## The rationale

One compact paragraph that teaches the deciding distinction: why the answer meets the constraint, and — when it helps — why the most tempting distractor does not. It reasons about ideas, never about positions or letters. Citations stay in the lesson's references. An explanation that needs several paragraphs belongs in the lesson; propose that edit when it is in scope.

## Item gates

An item is accepted only when all eight hold:

1. It assesses an identifiable decision inside the scope and the knowledge boundary.
2. Its claims and its answer are supported by primary sources, not by blueprint wording alone.
3. Every fact needed to answer is taught in the materials in scope (`materials`) or supported by an official source within the objectives (`exam`).
4. Exactly one answer is best under conditions stated in the prompt.
5. Every distractor is plausible and maps to a named mistake.
6. Read with the prompt covered, no choice is the odd one out, and wording, grammar and formatting give nothing else away.
7. The rationale teaches the deciding distinction and introduces nothing unsupported.
8. It conforms to the project's quiz contract.

Rewrite a failing item once. If it still fails, drop it — a shorter set of sound items beats a longer one.

## Set review

- **Distinct decisions.** A new company name on the same decision is a duplicate. Two items on one concept stay only when they turn on different constraints or failure modes.
- **Independence.** No prompt or revealed rationale answers another item.
- **Answer positions** vary without a pattern; a short set need not balance them.
- **Synthesis** for a checkpoint: questions that need two or more lessons together, alongside the local distinctions worth keeping — never the lessons' own quizzes copied across.
- **Coverage before variants.** Cover the decisions the scope supports first; stop when the next item would need trivia, filler, an unsupported fact or a contrived scenario, and report the gap instead.
